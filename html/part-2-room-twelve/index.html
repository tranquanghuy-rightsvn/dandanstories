Part 2 — Room Twelve

Nov 24, 2026

“Where did you take her?” Vic asked again.

I’d known him through two heart surgeries, a shop fire, and the winter a driver rolled one of our trucks into the Red River. I’d never heard him ask a question so quietly.

“The truck stop motel. They’re in room twelve.”

He reached for his coat. One sleeve had turned inside out when he took it off, and he kept trying to put his arm through it.

“Did she tell you where she was going?”

“I didn’t ask.”

“Did she say anything about me?”

“She asked if I could turn the heat up.”

He took his arm out of the sleeve. Outside the office window, the plow had pushed a ridge of snow across the entrance to the yard. The light above the fuel pump was still on.

“I told her to call her husband,” he said.

I remembered the voice on the dispatch line. I don’t have a husband anymore.

“Let me call her first,” I said. “She’s had a long night.”

For a second I thought he’d argue. He sat down in his desk chair and laid the call sheet flat in front of him. One corner kept curling up. He pressed it down with his thumb.

I called the motel. The clerk put me through, and April picked up on the fifth ring.

“Everything all right?” I asked.

“Sage is asleep. Bodhi’s trying to get the weather man to say something different.” In the background a boy said, “He keeps doing the same one.”

“April, your dad saw the call sheet. He wants to come over.”

There was a silence long enough for me to hear the television announcer name every county between us and Fargo.

“Did he fire you?”

I looked at Vic. The office was small. He could hear her through the phone.

“Yeah,” I said.

“Tell him he can come. I’ll meet him in the lobby.” She paused. “Will you come too?”

“I will.”

By the time we got there, the motel clerk had set out coffee and a sleeve of powdered doughnuts. Three truckers sat in front of the television with their coats on. Somebody had put a string of colored lights around the vending machine. Half the bulbs had burned out.

April was sitting on the end of a vinyl couch. She’d put her boots under the heating vent to dry. In the daylight I could see how young she still was. I’d last seen her at the shop at twenty-three, standing on a chair to change the battery in the smoke detector because she was tired of hearing it chirp.

Vic took off his hat.

“Hi, honey.”

She tucked her feet under the couch. Her socks didn’t match.

“Did you know it was me?” she asked.

“No. I didn’t.”

“I knew it was you when you said the rate.” She looked past him toward the motel doors. “I remembered the number off that magnet we used to hand out at the county fair. I thought you’d have somebody answering for you by now.”

“I could’ve come and gotten you myself.”

“I know. I sat there for a while with the phone in my hand. I thought if I said ‘Dad,’ you might come.” She rubbed her nose with the heel of her hand. “I couldn’t stand to hear you say no again.”

Vic turned his hat around until the bill was pointing at him.

“What happened to Matt?” he asked.

“He died in August.”

One of the truckers laughed at something on the television. The clerk turned down the volume.

“He went out to get milk,” April said. “Bodhi wanted pancakes in the morning. A guy crossed the center line.” She picked a loose thread from the cuff of her sweater. “For weeks I kept finding reasons to leave the porch light on. The electric bill came, and I got mad at myself for caring what it cost.”

Vic sat down in the chair across from her. “Somebody should’ve told me.”

“I called you the day after. You sent it to voicemail.” She let the thread fall to the carpet. “I called again after the funeral. I watched it ring twice and stop.”

He opened his mouth. I could almost hear what he’d been about to say. He screened calls when he was with a customer. His phone was always going off. He’d figured she would leave a message if it mattered.

“I saw your name,” he said finally. “Both times. I was still mad.”

April nodded once and looked down at her socks.

A door opened along the ground-floor hallway. Bodhi came out in dinosaur pajamas, dragging a blanket behind him. He stopped when he saw me.

“Is our car dead?”

“I think I can get it running when it warms up.”

“Mom’s purse is in it.”

“I brought your purse inside last night, bud,” April said.

“The other one. Dad’s.”

April closed her eyes for a second. “His lunch bag.”

The boy nodded. April told him she’d come get it with him later, and he climbed up beside her on the couch. He looked at Vic the way kids look at a man who might sit next to them on a bus.

“Bodhi, this is my dad,” April said. “His name’s Vic.”

“Where’s your dad?” Bodhi asked him.

Vic held his hat between his knees. “He’s been gone a long time.”

“Mine too,” Bodhi said. He gathered up the corner of the blanket and began trying to work it between his toes.

Vic watched him. After a little while, he put his hat on the floor.

“I’m sorry I left you out there,” he said to April. “I heard you say you had kids in the car. I heard you say the engine was dead. That was enough for me to go.”

April pulled the blanket up over Bodhi’s feet.

“Ernie nearly lost his job getting there.”

“He did lose it,” Vic said. He looked over at me. “I’d like you back.”

“I’m going to finish helping April first.”

He nodded.

“And the next parent who calls with kids in the car?”

He looked at his phone. I could see him doing what he always did, working through the cost of a thing before he opened his mouth. I used to think he’d worked like that all his life. But there had been years when the first thing he said after a call was, You know where that is, or you want me to ride with you?

“I’ll answer the line myself today,” he said. “If somebody’s stranded, I can figure out the bill after I get them inside.”

At the desk he took out his card to pay for April’s room. The clerk told him I’d paid through checkout. He asked for another night, looked down the hallway at April, and added a second room.

“If she wants me around, I’ll be close,” he told me.

I went out to the red car for the lunch bag. It took both hands to get the back door open. The thin blanket the kids had been under was still balled up on the seat, frozen stiff where somebody had spilled a drink on it. Underneath was a blue insulated bag with a broken zipper. When I carried it inside, Bodhi took it in both arms.

“He brought me cookies in it sometimes,” he said. “From work.”

There was an apple in the bag and a clean pair of Sage’s socks. April said Bodhi had packed it himself for the ride.

Vic came back from the desk with four paper cups of coffee, then looked at Bodhi and realized his mistake. The boy took one anyway. He liked how warm it felt through the paper. Vic showed him how to hold it with both hands.

April leaned toward me.

“Is Ruth at home?”

I’d heard people say the dead go on living in the minds of everyone who hasn’t heard the news. Until that moment I’d never understood what a mercy those few extra seconds could be. April was looking at me, waiting to hear what Ruth had planned for Christmas.

“She died in April,” I said. Saying the month to a woman with the same name felt strange. I’d been saying the spring for most of the year. “Her heart. I was with her.”

April’s fingers went to the little gold chain at her neck. She held it until she could speak.

“She wrote me in March,” she said. “She said she’d been getting tired in the afternoons. I thought she meant she was getting older.”

“So did I.”

She asked if Ruth had been able to talk to me at the end. I told her yes. Ruth’s last full sentence had been to ask whether I’d fed the cat. The cat had been dead for eleven years. I’d told her I’d just filled his bowl. She’d squeezed my hand, satisfied that somebody was keeping track.

April laughed, and then she put both hands over her face. I sat beside her until she took them down.

“I was headed to your house last night,” she said. “I’d planned to get in before bedtime. We had to stop twice for Sage, and I called the roads number before we left the gas station. By the time the snow got that bad I couldn’t see a driveway to turn around in.”

“You could’ve called me.”

“I know. Ruth gave me your number years ago.” She watched Bodhi blowing across the lid of Vic’s coffee. “I practiced what I’d say when I knocked. I was going to ask if you still had the tree with the electric train under it. That seemed like a thing I could say without crying.”

“We still have it,” I told her. It was in a box under the basement stairs. I hadn’t taken it out that year.

Sage woke around ten. She emerged from the room with a damp curl stuck to her cheek and announced that her boots were gone. Vic got down on one knee and showed her where they were drying. She thought about it, took one boot, and asked him to move over so she could sit where he was kneeling. He moved.

A little later the dispatch line rang. His hand went to his pocket fast enough that I figured he’d been waiting for it.

A woman had slid up against a curb a few blocks from the motel. She had her father with her. They could walk inside the grocery store to warm up, she said, but she didn’t know how she’d pay for a tow.

“Go inside with your dad,” Vic said. “I’ll call the store when I get there.”

He hung up, put his hat on, and turned to me.

“Would you come with me? I’d have to get the truck first.”

“I’ll be here when you get back.”

It took him a moment. Then he looked at April, who was unwrapping a powdered doughnut for Sage.

“Is that all right with you?” he asked her. “If I come back?”

She shook the doughnut out onto a napkin. “I told Bodhi we’d eat lunch at noon. You could bring something.”

“What does he like?”

“Grilled cheese with the crust on. You have to tell him it’s on there on purpose.”

Vic wrote it on the back of the motel receipt.

By eleven, the snow was coming down lightly enough that I could see the service road from the lobby. The truckers went out one at a time to check their rigs. The clerk brought two rolls of quarters from the office so Bodhi could turn the crank on the gumball machine. He got a yellow one, his least favorite, and put it in his father’s lunch bag to save.

April came out with her coat over her arm. She reached into the inside pocket and took out a brown envelope. A corner had gone soft from getting wet.

“This was the other reason I was coming,” she said.

My name was on the front in Ruth’s handwriting. Even after she’d started using a magnifying glass to read the newspaper, she’d written the way she used to label the jars in our pantry, big enough to see from across the kitchen.

“She mailed that to you?”

“Inside another envelope. She asked me to bring it myself when I was ready to come home.” April gave me a tired little smile. “I almost opened it at the gas station. I figured if I’d waited this many years, I could wait another hour.”

Inside was a photograph of Ruth and Vic on the day we got our first truck. Vic had a mustache then. Ruth was holding a paper cup, squinting into the sun. Through the open garage door I could see the corner of the desk Vic still used. I remembered taking the picture. Afterward I’d gone to find a rag to wipe the grease off the seat so Ruth could be the first passenger.

Behind the photo was a copy of a check, an agreement with two signatures, and a sheet of lined paper folded in half.

I recognized the amount on the check. Twelve thousand dollars. Ruth’s father had left it to her when he died. She told me she’d put it somewhere she thought it would do some good. I’d assumed it was in a bank all these years. Every time we needed a new roof or a furnace, I figured she’d tell me if she wanted to use it.

I read the agreement twice. It gave Ruth half the towing company in exchange for the money to buy the truck. There was Vic’s signature, as plain as it had been on every paycheck he’d handed me for twenty-eight years.

Ruth’s letter was short.

Ernie,

Vic couldn’t get the loan, and you’d already given notice at the mill. I didn’t want either of you finding that out from the other. So I bought in. I asked Vic to keep it between us until we knew whether we could make a go of it. After that there always seemed to be a reason to put off telling you.

I signed papers to leave my share to you. They’re with the lawyer. I should have told you myself at the kitchen table. I sat there with you last night while you peeled an orange for me, and I still couldn’t make myself start.

Ask Vic to show you the books. Start with the winter you took those three sisters home to Larimore. He put every one of those tows you said you’d done for free under my name. I didn’t mind paying. I want you to see what he put under his.

Love, Ruth

I read the line about the orange again. Her fingers had been too sore to get under the peel. I used to leave a little pile of the white strings on the table, and she used to swipe them into her palm before I’d finished eating my half.

“He fired me from Ruth’s company,” I said.

It came out almost like a laugh. April sat down beside me. She let me hold the letter open on my knee for a while.

The tow truck pulled up just before noon. Vic stepped down with snow packed around his bootlaces and a white paper bag tucked against his chest. Bodhi ran to tell him about the yellow gumball. Vic handed him the bag and said there were four sandwiches in it, and the cook had left the crust on one especially for him.

Then he saw the photograph on my knee.

“Ruth sent it to me,” April told him.

He came over slowly and lowered himself into the same chair he’d sat in that morning.

“You could’ve told me any day since April,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you?”

He looked toward Bodhi. The boy had laid the sandwiches out on the coffee table and was asking his mother if he could give Sage the little paper cup of pickles.

“There’s a man coming to the shop at noon tomorrow,” Vic said. “He was meant to come today, but he’s stuck in Fargo.”

“Who is he?”

“The man I sold the company to.”

I looked down at Ruth’s signature. “Your half?”

Vic rubbed a wet spot on the knee of his jeans. For twenty-eight winters I’d seen him do the same thing after coming in from the yard.

“He thinks he bought all of it,” he said. “I told him you’d sign when he got here.”

Outside, the motel sign gave a little shudder in the wind. Bodhi set a wrapped sandwich beside his grandfather’s hat and went back to the couch.

“Where’s the money, Vic?” I asked.

He put a hand on the sandwich, feeling the heat through the wrapper.

“Come to the shop with me, Ernie,” he said. “Before I tell you, there’s something you need to see in Ruth’s books.”
