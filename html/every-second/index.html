Every Second

CHAPTER 1 - Every Second

PART 1: The Man on the Driveway

The first thing I saw when I looked down the barrel of Officer Daniel Whitmore’s gun was that his hand was shaking.

“Get your hands where I can see them! On your knees, right now!”

I had a cup of coffee in my right hand. It was a white mug with a hairline crack in the handle, the only one I’d bothered to unpack. I remember looking down at it and wondering which would scare him more, setting it on the ground or letting it fall.

“I’m holding a mug,” I said. “I’m going to put it down.”

“Drop it!”

It shattered between my shoes. Hot coffee splashed my ankles. I raised both hands and spread my fingers as far apart as they would go.

Across the driveway, my new neighbor, Victoria Langford, was crying into one hand. Her expensive cream coat hung open at the throat, torn at the collar. A lock of hair had been pulled loose and lay across her cheek. She had looked ready for a photograph ten minutes earlier. Now she looked like someone who’d had to fight her way out of a locked room.

“He tried to kill me,” she said. “He grabbed my neck. He tried to drag me into his garage.”

Whitmore glanced at her collar. I saw how quickly that small piece of torn fabric settled something for him.

“He said people like me don’t belong here,” Victoria added.

That was when I understood how much thought she’d put into it. She’d taken the ugliest thing she had said to me and put it in my mouth.

My name is Marcus Reed. The driveway we were standing on belonged to me. So did the dark sedan parked behind me, the garage Victoria claimed I’d tried to drag her into, and the house I’d spent six weeks trying to close on. The previous evening, I’d carried the last of my boxes inside. That morning, I’d gone out to drink a cup of coffee in the sun and look at the place without a moving truck in front of it.

She’d crossed the cul-de-sac while I was clearing flattened boxes from beside the garage. When I heard the click of her shoes, I looked up and smiled. I’d been meaning to introduce myself.

“Good morning,” I said.

“Who let you in?”

I thought she meant through the neighborhood gate.

“The same code the movers used. I live here. I’m Marcus.”

She looked past me, into the garage. “Where are the owners?”

“You’re talking to one.”

She gave a small laugh, then looked at me again, waiting for me to laugh with her. When I didn’t, she asked to see my closing papers.

I could have gone inside and got them. They were still in a folder on the kitchen counter, along with a receipt for the additional set of keys I’d had cut. For half a second, I pictured myself standing there with the folder, watching her run a finger down every page, deciding what counted as proof.

“Who are you?” I asked.

“Victoria Langford. I live across the street. I know who lives in this neighborhood.”

“Then you’ve met your new neighbor.”

I folded another box. She stepped over the seam where the pavement met my driveway.

“A Black man shows up in a house that’s been sitting empty, and I’m supposed to just take his word for it?”

I stopped folding.

“Are you asking if I bought the house,” I said, “or are you saying I broke into it?”

“Squatters have answers for everything.”

I pulled out my phone and held it up. I meant for her to see I was recording. I thought it would make her hear herself.

Her eyes went straight to the screen. “Put that away.”

“Please get off my driveway.”

She came at the phone so fast I barely got it out of reach. Her hand swept past my knuckles. I stepped back. She reached again, and I moved around the front of my car, keeping enough space between us that she couldn’t grab it. On the third step my heel caught the bottom of a cardboard box. I heard it crunch and thought how ridiculous it would be to fall over my own moving boxes on my first morning home.

“Victoria.” I’d raised my voice by then. “Go home.”

She stopped just short of the garage. I thought she was going to come at me again. Instead she looked toward the nearest house, where a blind had moved in an upstairs window. Then she fixed her coat, turned around, and walked away.

I watched her as far as the sidewalk. I set my phone on the hood of the car and went inside for the coffee I’d poured before coming out. My hand was shaking a little when I picked up the mug. I stood at the kitchen counter and told myself that I’d had one bad encounter with one neighbor. I could still have a good morning in my own house.

Five minutes later the cruiser came around the bend. I remember thinking the officer might ask me where Victoria lived.

Now his gun was pointed at my chest.

“Officer, I own this house,” I said. “I never touched her.”

“Shut your mouth.”

He came in so fast that all I saw was the change in his shoulders. He caught my right arm and twisted it behind me. My cheek struck the hood of my car. The sound was dull and surprisingly loud, like someone had dropped something heavy in an empty room.

“Daniel, wait,” a woman called. “Let’s assess the situation first.”

I caught sight of her reflection in the windshield as she came around the cruiser. Her nameplate read COLLINS. She had one hand raised toward her partner. He was already pulling my other arm behind me.

“He’s a violent assault suspect, Sarah. Look at her.”

The first cuff closed. He squeezed it again, two sharp clicks after the metal was already tight. The second went on harder. I turned my head to breathe and saw, just past the edge of the windshield, the black camera below the corner of the garage roof.

“Look up,” I said. “I have two cameras. They recorded everything.”

Collins looked.

Whitmore drove his knee into my back.

And Victoria, who’d been looking at the same camera, suddenly screamed that I had a knife.

PART 2: Keep Your Hands Still

“He’s reaching into his pocket!”

For one absurd second, I wondered whose hands she could possibly be looking at. Both of mine were locked behind my back, high enough that my shoulders burned. I could feel the empty air between my fingers. I’d been concentrating on keeping them open even after the cuffs went on.

Whitmore shifted his weight. Something hard pressed into the base of my neck.

“Move and I’ll tase you.”

My whole body wanted to pull away from the pressure. I flattened myself against the hood instead. There was a tiny chip in the paint about an inch from my left eye. I tried to keep that chip exactly where it was.

“Do you see a knife?” Collins asked.

“She just said he has one.”

“I’m asking what you see.”

The thing against my neck stayed there. I heard Victoria take several quick breaths. Then, for the first time since the cruiser arrived, nobody spoke. A sprinkler clicked on two houses down. I remember being furious at the sound. Some timer was going to finish its morning cycle whether I got up off that car or stayed there.

“I can see his hands,” Collins said. “They’re both cuffed. Daniel, take the Taser off his neck.”

“He was about to reach.”

“His hands have been behind him the entire time I’ve been standing here.”

The pressure lifted. It took me another second to trust that it was gone.

Collins came close enough for me to see the toes of her boots. “Mr. Reed, is there anything sharp in your pockets?”

“No.” My voice came out rough. “Keys on the left. Wallet on the right. My phone’s on the hood.”

I could see it above the wiper, where I’d set it down before I went inside. I’d stuck a light blue case on it because I kept losing it in the dark. I’d never been happier to see that stupid case.

“Both cameras go to that phone,” I said. “The garage camera looks straight down the driveway. The one over the porch points across it. Start with the garage. You can hear her.”

Victoria cut in. “He had the phone out the whole time. He could have filmed whatever he wanted you to see.”

“I know when I picked up the phone,” I said. “The cameras were rolling before you got here.”

She’d cried hard enough to redden her eyes. I saw them change as she looked at me. The tears were still there, and underneath them was the same sharp irritation I’d seen when I told her she could leave without seeing my papers.

“He’s trying to intimidate me,” she said.

Collins turned toward her. “Ms. Langford, you can wait by the cruiser. I’ll come speak to you in a minute.”

“I’d like him taken away first.”

“I need you by the cruiser.”

Victoria went. She took a wide path around the broken mug, lifting the hem of her coat clear of the coffee. Whitmore kept a hand on my upper arm. With his knee off my back, I felt every separate place he’d hurt me. My left wrist was the worst. The fingers on that hand had started to tingle.

“Could you loosen the cuffs?” I asked.

“Stay still,” he said.

“I am still. My fingers are going numb.”

Collins looked down at my wrists. “Daniel.”

He let out a breath through his nose. Then he eased the left cuff one click. The relief came hard and fast enough to make my eyes sting.

I could have told him exactly what was in my wallet. I’d almost said it when Collins asked about my pockets. There was a line I could say, a title, a way of making him hear my voice differently. I wanted to say it. I wanted his hand off my arm, and I wanted back the certainty I’d had twenty minutes before that I could stand outside my own garage without getting hurt.

I also remembered what he’d done when Victoria said knife. My wallet could stay where it was until somebody was ready to listen.

Collins picked up her radio and asked for a supervisor. She gave our address, said there’d been a use of force, and requested a unit for a second statement. Whitmore let go of my arm only long enough to turn toward her.

“We can sort this out ourselves,” he said.

“I’ve asked for a supervisor,” she said, and clipped the radio back on.

I had spent the previous night testing the new cameras while I was supposed to be unpacking. The installer had stood at the curb and waved while I moved the porch camera a fraction to the left from my phone. I’d asked if I really needed that much of the cul-de-sac in the frame. He said it would make it easier to see where anyone on the driveway had come from. In the end I’d left it there because I could see the whole passenger side of my car. I’d had a mirror clipped once in a parking lot. That was the kind of trouble I’d bought the cameras for.

“May I open the camera app?” Collins asked.

“Yes. Hold it up to my face. It should unlock.”

She brought the phone down to my level. My cheek was red in the little reflection in the glass. I raised my chin just enough for the screen to recognize me.

“The green icon on the bottom row,” I said. “There’s one marked Garage.”

A list of clips loaded. She tapped the most recent one, and we heard the scrape of her own boots on the pavement. She went back. The next clip had the cruiser coming in. She went back again and stopped on a thumbnail of Victoria crossing my driveway.

By the cruiser, Victoria spoke up. “I may have gotten confused about exactly where he grabbed me. I was terrified. You have to understand that.”

Collins had been about to touch the screen. She held her thumb still and looked over.

“Where do you remember him grabbing you?”

“My neck. From behind. It all happened so fast.”

“You told my partner he came at you while you were walking past the house.”

“Yes. That’s what I’m saying.”

Collins tapped the clip. From the tiny speaker came the sound of cardboard scraping over concrete, and then my own voice, easy and friendly in a way I could scarcely remember.

“Good morning.”

A second later, Victoria heard herself ask who had let me in.

PART 3: From the Other Side

I couldn’t see much of the phone from where Whitmore held me. I caught small patches of movement whenever Collins angled the screen against the sun. My own voice sounded farther away than Victoria’s. She’d been standing closer to the garage wall, and the microphone had picked up every word.

“Where are the owners?”

“You’re talking to one.”

A breeze lifted a corner of cardboard in the video. It made the same dry ticking sound I’d heard while Victoria waited for me to explain myself. Listening to it again, I remembered how badly I’d wanted to get back to my boxes. I had half a house to unpack. I’d still been hoping we’d get through the awkward first minute and become the kind of neighbors who waved.

The woman beside the cruiser folded her arms over the torn collar.

“That’s before,” she said. “Keep watching.”

Collins did.

When Victoria said the words “A Black man shows up,” Whitmore turned his head. He didn’t turn it toward me. He looked across the driveway at the woman he had taken at her word.

I heard myself ask what she was accusing me of. I heard her answer. Then came the part I remembered most clearly, the swish of her sleeve when she went for the phone and the crunch of the box under my heel.

“Go back five seconds,” I told Collins. “Watch my feet.”

She slid the clip back. This time she held the phone where Whitmore could see.

From that angle, my shoulders were square to Victoria. For a couple of frames, her right hand disappeared behind mine. You could tell from my feet that I was backing away. You could also see the exact moment the front of the car blocked the camera’s view of our legs. Victoria spotted it as soon as the officer did.

“There,” she said. “He pulled me in right after that.”

I remembered how hard I’d tried to keep clear of her, even when I nearly tripped. Hearing her say it while I could still feel the cardboard give under my heel made me want to yell. I knew how that would sound to the man holding me. I waited until I could get the words out evenly.

“Open the porch camera.”

Collins switched views. The porch camera showed the driveway from the house side, across the front of the car and out to the street. I asked her to line the clips up on the sound of the box crushing. It took her two tries. Then I could see myself from the side, my heel half buried in cardboard, phone in my right hand and my left hand hanging open by my hip. Victoria reached again. I backed one more step. There was enough pavement between us to see sunlight beneath both our shadows.

Collins played that part twice. Then she let it run.

On-screen, Victoria looked toward the neighboring house and walked down the driveway. I disappeared through the side door that led to the kitchen. The camera followed neither of us. It stayed pointed exactly where I’d left it, which meant it still had Victoria in frame when she reached her mailbox across the street.

She stopped beside it. For a second, she was just a woman looking back at a house.

Then she reached up with both hands and tore the collar of her coat.

The sound barely carried. I heard the fabric give once, and then again when she pulled harder. She let the damaged side hang open and reached for her phone. The motion clip ran out with her standing there, looking straight at my garage.

Even Whitmore said nothing.

I lifted my head enough to see the actual mailbox. It was black with brass numbers. Her coat had caught against it for a second in the video, and a pale thread still clung to one corner. I’d been standing here all this time faced toward that house. I’d never have known to look.

“I snagged it,” Victoria said. “I was trying to get it loose.”

Collins moved the clip back and played it again. Victoria watched for about a second before she turned away.

“Do you still believe her?” I asked Whitmore.

His fingers loosened on my arm. He looked down at my wrists as though he’d just remembered how long he’d held them there.

“Daniel,” Collins said, “turn him loose.”

He fumbled the key once against the first lock. When the cuff opened, I brought my arm forward slowly, keeping my elbow close to my side. The other cuff came free. I stood up an inch at a time because the driveway felt like it might tilt if I moved too fast.

Collins asked if I needed an ambulance. I told her I wanted my hands checked. My left fingertips felt as though I’d left them out in the cold.

I heard tires slow on the road behind me. A black SUV rolled into the cul-de-sac and stopped short of the cruiser. A woman in a dark uniform stepped out, put on her cap, and looked from the broken mug to the cuffs still in Whitmore’s hand.

“Lieutenant Ruiz,” Collins said. “I called for you.”

Ruiz took one look at my wrists. “Who put him in cuffs?”

“I did,” Whitmore said. “We had a report of an assault.”

Collins held up my phone. “We have both angles on video. I’ve watched them through.”

Ruiz asked if she could see. I nodded. Collins stood beside her and ran the clips from the beginning. Victoria took a step toward the two women when the sound started, then seemed to think better of it. By the time the porch clip ended, she’d backed up until her coat brushed the cruiser.

Ruiz handed me my phone. “I want those clips preserved. Will you send us both originals?”

“Yes. And I’d like to make a statement while I can still tell you exactly what happened.”

“I’ll take it myself. Can I get your full name?”

“Marcus Reed. My identification is in my right front pocket.” I said it loud enough for Whitmore to hear. “I’m going to reach in and get it.”

The three of them watched my right hand as I pulled out my wallet.

I flipped it open. On one side was my county identification. On the other, set into worn black leather, was my official badge.

PART 4: What He Saw

Ruiz read the card. “Detective Marcus Reed. County Sheriff’s Office. Professional Standards.”

I remember the way Whitmore looked at me then. He’d been close enough to feel me breathing while he held me down. He’d had plenty of chances to see my face. Now his eyes went from the wallet to my cheek, and he looked stricken.

“Professional Standards?” he said.

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

The question came out of him before he’d had time to think about it. I could hear the honest confusion in it, and that made me angrier than anything he could have said on purpose.

“I told you I lived here,” I said. “I told you there were cameras. You told me to shut my mouth.”

He looked at the junior officer beside him. Collins held his gaze. She had heard him say it too.

I closed the wallet. My left hand could scarcely grip the leather. “When she said I had a knife, I’d already told you where the proof was. What did you see that made you believe I was reaching?”

Whitmore looked at the hood of the car. There was a smear where my cheek had been. “I heard her yell,” he said.

I waited.

“I didn’t see you reach.”

He said it more quietly than he’d said anything all morning. I needed Ruiz to hear it, and from the way she turned toward him, I knew she had.

“Give Officer Collins the cuffs,” Ruiz said. “Then wait in my vehicle.”

He handed them over. One open cuff swung and caught against the other with a little click. I flinched. He saw it. His mouth moved once, as if he’d been about to say my name. Then he went to the SUV.

Victoria was still standing by the cruiser. The moment she saw me look her way, she pulled the two sides of her collar together.

“If I’d known who you were, I never would have called,” she said.

“You could have asked my name.”

“I was looking out for the neighborhood.”

“I did tell you my name. It was the first thing I said after I told you I lived here.”

For a second I thought she might remember it the way I did, with my hand still on the cardboard and a smile on my face. Then she looked down at the torn cloth between her fingers and asked if she could go home.

“Stay here,” Ruiz said. “Officer Collins will speak with you.”

Ruiz offered me a seat on the porch while we waited for someone to check my hands. I said yes and started toward the front walk. My legs were steady through the first few steps, then I hit the same seam Victoria had stepped over and had to put my good hand on the fender. Collins saw me stop. She stayed where she was and gave me the moment to get my feet under me. I appreciated that more than I knew how to tell her.

On the front step, Ruiz asked me to start with when I first saw Victoria. I told her about the moving boxes, the demand for papers, and the exact words I could remember. I told her that I’d held up my phone because I wanted Victoria to see it. I told her I’d raised my voice. I could feel myself wanting to skip that last detail because I knew how easily somebody could use it. I said it anyway. I wanted the account to survive being held up against every second of the video.

When I got to the cruiser pulling in, I lost my place. I’d told people before that memory can come back in pieces after something frightening. I knew to sit and let them come. On my own front step, with an officer waiting for me to finish a sentence, I found out how hard it was to take that advice. For a while all I could remember was trying to figure out where to put my coffee.

“He told me to drop it,” I said eventually. “I dropped it.”

Ruiz wrote that down.

A medic checked the swelling near my cheekbone and the feeling in my fingers. I could move all of them by then. While he packed up, I sent Ruiz the original clips. She asked which ones showed the confrontation, and my thumb landed on the file that started with the cruiser. I heard Whitmore’s first shout before I could hit pause. Across the driveway, Collins looked up. She’d been taking Victoria’s statement far enough away to give us privacy. I wondered what it had been like for her to hear that shout and realize it had come from the man she’d arrived with.

“Include that one too,” I told Ruiz. “And the other angle for the same time.”

I knew both reports would start with an allegation of assault. It was the reason the cruiser had come to the house. I wanted whoever read those reports to see what the police had done once they arrived. I wanted them to hear Collins asking him to wait. I wanted them to be able to count how long he kept the Taser against my neck after she told him she could see both my hands.

“Are you filing a formal complaint?” Ruiz asked.

“Yes.”

I’d spent years across a desk from people making that choice. Some had shown up with photographs and some had walked in with nothing but their own account. I remembered how often people asked me, before they sat down, whether I thought coming in would make a difference. I used to say that I’d listen to what happened and go where the facts took me. I still believed in doing that. Sitting on my step, I understood why some of them hadn’t found it reassuring.

A second patrol car arrived. Collins conferred with Ruiz and then went back to Victoria. I could hear her from where I sat when she told Victoria she was under arrest for making a false report. Victoria looked toward her house, as if she expected the sight of her own front door to settle the matter.

“I told you I was scared,” she said.

Collins asked her to keep her hands where she could see them. Her voice was calm. She waited for Victoria to comply.

I watched from my step until the cruiser left. Then I took my wallet out again because I needed my key from the same pocket. The badge caught the light once before I put it away. I thought about how long it had taken Whitmore to ask himself whether he’d made a mistake, and how close I’d come to paying for those minutes.

When I went inside, I left the front door open. I could still see the driveway from the kitchen, and for the rest of the morning I wanted to know who was on it.

PART 5: My Own Front Door

That evening I went out with a broom and swept up the mug. The coffee had dried into a dark stain, and a little white crescent from the handle had skidded all the way under the front tire. I had to back the car up to get it. When the engine started, both cameras sent an alert to my phone. I sat with the car in park until the screen went dark.

For the first week, I answered every notification. A delivery driver came up the walk with a package for the previous owner. A cat crossed the driveway at two in the morning. A gust of wind pushed the trash bin far enough to put a wheel in the garage camera’s field of view. I opened each clip and watched it to the end. One night I got up after the phone buzzed and found myself standing in the living room with the lights off, waiting to see headlights come around the bend.

I’d wanted to be able to sit in the house and feel that it was mine. I could do the ordinary things. I unpacked plates. I learned which light switch controlled the porch. I got a paint sample for the kitchen and put a little square of it on the wall. Then I’d walk out to get the mail and catch myself holding both hands away from my body. My skin healed faster than that habit did.

Some neighbors began to wave again. One man told me he’d heard the siren and assumed someone had had a medical emergency. A woman two doors down said she’d seen me against the hood from her upstairs window. She’d thought the officer must have had a reason. She told me that as if she’d been waiting to get it off her chest. I said I’d thought about that window too. It was the one Victoria had looked up at before she walked away.

My phone rang about two weeks after the incident. It was Collins. She said she’d given her formal account and wanted me to know she’d described what she saw from the moment she stepped out of the cruiser.

“I put in that you told us about the cameras before she called out about the knife,” she said.

“That’s how I remember it.”

She went quiet. Then she said, “I wish I’d gotten between you two sooner.”

I remembered her boots coming into view beside the hood. I remembered how long a second had felt with the Taser at my neck.

“I wish you had too,” I said. “I’m glad you stayed with it.”

“So am I,” she said.

By then another investigator had taken a fuller statement from me. I brought the clip from each camera and sat through them both. There was a moment in the garage footage during the arrest when my fingers stretched open behind my back even as Whitmore pushed down on me. I hadn’t known the camera could see them that well. I asked the investigator to pause there. The image was sharp enough to see the space between every finger.

“Right there,” I said. “That’s when she says I’m reaching.”

We watched the rest without stopping. At the end he asked if there was anything else I wanted in my statement. I’d thought there would be dozens of things I’d want to say. The only one I could think of was the thing I’d been trying to tell Whitmore from the beginning.

“I was on my own driveway,” I said.

The department sustained my complaint about the use of force. Ruiz called after the review was complete to tell me Whitmore had been dismissed. I asked her whether he had acknowledged what he’d said in front of us that morning. She told me he’d stuck with it. He’d heard Victoria claim I was reaching. He’d never seen me move toward a pocket.

His acknowledgment mattered to me. I’d spent part of the first week replaying the way he’d asked why I hadn’t identified myself, as if I could have made things go better by getting the right words out sooner. I could finally think about that morning without drafting a better answer for him. I’d spoken plainly. He’d made his choice.

Victoria pleaded guilty to making the false report. I was in the room when she admitted I had never put my hands on her. She said it in a low, flat voice. I’d thought I might feel a rush of satisfaction when I heard her say it. What came instead was a long breath I didn’t know I’d been holding. I had spent months hearing her scream that I’d tried to kill her. I wanted to remember the sound of her voice saying this too.

Afterward I saw her in the hallway. For a moment it was just the two of us, close enough for either of us to speak. Her gaze dropped to my hands. Then she stepped aside to let me pass.

The first morning warm enough to leave the front door open again, I took a cup of coffee outside. I’d bought a new mug weeks before, but until then I’d been drinking from it in the kitchen. I walked past the front step and stood where I’d been when the cruiser pulled in. For a moment I could see the whole encounter laid out around me. The broken mug at my feet. Whitmore coming straight at me. Victoria standing where the sun hit her torn collar. The painted hood was warm under my palm.

A garage door opened somewhere across the street. My shoulder tightened before I even looked. A neighbor was dragging his trash bin out to the curb in slippers. He saw me, lifted a hand, and went back for the recycling. I lifted my mug to him.

My phone buzzed. The garage camera had picked me up when I came outside. Through the little window in the notification I could see myself with the coffee in one hand and the front door open behind me. I put the phone back in my pocket without opening the clip.

Then I sat down on my front step and drank my coffee while it was still hot.
